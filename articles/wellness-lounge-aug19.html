Bandit put $1 million into a race instead of a media buy

Bandit started its events business with unsanctioned races run right outside the office on a $2,500 budget. The Bandit Grand Prix, now in its second year, costs about $1 million top line. Nick West studied music festival economics to work out how to do it without losing his shirt, then funded it the way a festival does: sponsors, tickets sold to racers, and Bandit's own merch.

The logic for spending it that way is the sharpest thing said all night. A million dollar media buy is a million dollars of selling. A million dollar race is a million dollars of experience, and the people inside it do the distributing.

Nine laps on a closed course, indoor and outdoor, with a DJ. Nick's own role tells you how the economics hold: he is in the venue building things himself so the company can hire one less person until the thing is proven.

"When you spend a million dollars on a media buy, everything that you're doing is selling."

Nick West, Co-founder, Bandit Running

Paid media is an accelerant, never an originator

Nick's framing: performance media cannot originate demand, it can only speed up demand that already exists. Bandit does not want a new customer's first contact to be a performance ad. It wants that first contact to be a long YouTube story about an unsponsored athlete going to the Olympics, or an in-person experience at a major marathon. The ad comes later, as the reminder.

Because so much of the spend is brand marketing, attribution gets directional at best, so Bandit watches blended CAC and runs one specific litmus test. Are organic purchases growing faster than performance marketing purchases? If paid ever starts growing faster, the read is simple: the brand is having to sell itself more often than customers are telling their friends about it.

Four years, zero conversion rate tests

For the first four years of the company, Bandit did not make a single conversion rate optimization change to its website. Not one. Every dollar that would have gone into A/B testing button colors went into making people care about the brand instead.

Nick is clear this was a stage-appropriate call, not a permanent principle. At a hundred million in revenue the math changes, because half a basis point of conversion on a million sessions is real money in a way it never is on ten thousand. Bandit does the optimization work now, and he credits AI for making most of those insights automatic.

"If we get people so obsessed with the brand, they're gonna figure out how to find the checkout."

Nick West, Co-founder, Bandit Running

Ankur's warning: you cannot decide to do community in two years

Ankur Goyal is CMO at Ultra, the country's largest nicotine free pouch brand. It was founded last year and has already sold more than two million cans. He spent over ten years in CPG before this, including scaling the diaper brand Coterie.

He refused the premise that Ultra has built a cult yet. Bandit is a cult brand. The Knicks are a cult brand. Twelve months in, his job is to describe what it looks like while it is being built, not to claim the finished thing.

His actual warning is about timing. Community takes years, which means the decision has to be made before you need it. You cannot choose in two years to have a two year old community.

"If you want a community two years from now, you kinda gotta think about it now."

Ankur Goyal, CMO, Ultra

What a real content engine actually costs

Ankur put a number on it, which almost nobody does. Ultra spends low six figures per month on content, covering agencies, production, headcount, and partnerships. That buys six to seven hundred pieces of content a month, and they expect to double it within two months.

The structure matters more than the volume. Several different streams feed the engine at once: influencers producing at scale, people on payroll who are good on camera, and deliberately expensive one-off shoots with partners like Larry Wheels and Rampage Jackson, who cost more than a brand Ultra's size would be expected to pay but produce something that actually breaks through. All of it lands in a pre and post production operation with fixed boxes to check.

His own summary of the job: be creative about what goes in, be an accountant about what comes out.

The Coterie vacation pack, and the service layer nobody builds

Ankur's best story is about diapers. Coterie noticed from its own data that its parents traveled far more than average. So it built the vacation pack: you can have a box of diapers shipped straight to your hotel and you pay the shipping. Not a giveaway, and cheaper than checking a bag.

The idea came out of reading CX logs, not a strategy offsite. Ankur's frame is that most brands think about product and brand as the two layers that build loyalty, and skip the third one sitting between them, which is the services you offer. Community is one of those services. Content is another. A box of diapers waiting at a hotel is another.

The Knicks get their own players for one day a year

Toby Gimand sells partnerships across the Knicks, Rangers, Rockettes, and MSG's live concerts, after about eight years at the NFL. The Knicks went fifty three years without a title before this season, and his point was that the rabid fan base never depended on the winning.

The constraint he named is one most brands never consider. MSG gets access to its own players for content exactly once a year, a single eight hour shoot day, and everything branded has to come out of it. That scarcity is why the growth is in creator programs instead. Their first ever beauty partner on the Knicks used the deal to host influencers: suites at games, shootaround access, a post-game photo on the court, things you cannot buy off the street.

He was also unusually honest about why big properties move slowly, and said the arrogance is real and not his alone. Seven figure deals, hyper protective of the brand, rarely first to try anything.

"We're typically not ever first to do things, because we don't have to be."

Toby Gimand, Madison Square Garden Entertainment

Acting small on purpose

Asked how Bandit stays cool while scaling, Nick gave the line of the night and then the mechanic behind it. The email list has gone from five thousand to half a million. The response is not to think about how much bigger the audience is, but to keep doing what worked at 140 fans, only better.

Concretely: after every single collection, Bandit brings 50 men and 50 women into its Brooklyn store and asks them what they liked and what they did not. Nick says that is never going to change. He also insists Bandit still has low brand awareness, which is the sort of thing founders stop saying right before they get complacent.

"The fastest way to kill a cult is to try to grow it."

Nick West, Co-founder, Bandit Running

What to take from the room

  • Run Nick's litmus test on your own numbers. If performance marketing purchases are growing faster than organic ones, you are buying growth your brand has not earned.
  • Bandit's event math only works with a near not-for-profit mentality up front. Optimize the inputs that create emotional engagement first, and the profitability follows in later years or not at all.
  • Ankur's service layer is the cheapest differentiator on this list. Read your CX logs, find the thing customers keep working around, and turn it into an offer. The vacation pack came from support tickets.
  • Put a real number on your content operation before you judge it. Low six figures a month buying 600 to 700 pieces is the benchmark Ultra shared openly.
  • On traditional media, Nick is bullish and launching linear TV, with radio possibly in Q1. His rule for a smaller budget: the idea beats the budget. Bandit ran a billboard that did not carry its own brand name.
  • Scarcity of access is a strategy problem, not just a constraint. MSG gets one eight hour day a year with its players and solved it by handing the storytelling to creators instead.
