The plan was $50K a year

Albert grew up in Gainesville, which is where Gatorade comes from. Ran track at Florida, drank the stuff constantly, got headaches.

So he started making his own. Then he made it for his teammates.

His teammates turned pro. Professional mix. Promix.

That is the whole origin, and it is a lot less romantic than people expect. Pre-med, food science, exercise physiology, registered dietitian, ten years as a trainer in New York.

"My dream was if I can make products I like and I can make 50K a year, then kind of 50K plus any type of salary, I'm gonna be fine, especially in Gainesville."

He set up his own production facility because he did not know any better. Built the website. Did the fulfillment. All of it.

He says that is exactly why he can hire and scale now. You cannot manage what you have never done.

GNC on one side, birdseed on the other

Picture the supplement aisle in 2013.

"What was available was basically GNC, mega-muscle, super artificial and synthetic, and then you had what I would call bird seed in the natural health food store."

Enormous white space in the middle. That gap is the entire company.

He started with protein because you cannot carry a piece of chicken in your bag all day. Grass-fed whey, when that was still a new idea. Then organic pea protein from Canadian peas for people who did not want dairy.

His rule on timing: get ahead of the market. If you can already see the wave, you are late.

He is building for the meaty 40%

This is the sharpest thing he said all night, and almost nobody frames it this way.

He is not chasing the 1%. Those people already have a personal trainer, he serviced exactly that clientele in New York for ten years.

"What I really wanted to hit was kind of the meaty 40% of the market that wants to kind of invest in their health, but they're not trying to jump this huge chasm to like super organic and everything."

Which is why taste is a formulation requirement, not a nice-to-have. The best compliment he gets is that it tastes good and you can still tell it is healthy.

The products he refuses to make

Ten years ago he looked hard at colostrum, back when it was very, very niche. The studies said you need a big dose, the cost made that dose impossible, so he passed.

Other companies shipped it at a level that lets them make a claim. That did not fit his filter.

Gummies, same answer. "I think we could sell gummies, but I don't think it fits with our framework."

His reasoning is blunt. Children eat gummies. The carrier needs ingredients he does not want in there, and Promix is trying to be taken clinically.

A filter that only says yes is a wish list.

One channel, all the way down

One of his advisers spent years at VaynerMedia and has watched brands at $400M, $500M and up, so he has seen where growth goes wrong.

His question landed hard.

"Do you understand how far you can go with the one channel? If you have one channel that's working, variety is not the answer at all."

Albert Matheny, quoting his adviser

Meta is working, so people go add TikTok and Snapchat. Albert's take is to do more of the thing already working until it genuinely runs out.

Promix has always stayed first order profitable, and they still spend more on organic marketing and events than on paid. When they do lift ad spend, they lift the community side with it so it feels even.

Why he keeps saying no to retail

"Retail is like death for most, for a lot of brands." Expensive, and the cashflow cycle is brutal.

The line he keeps came from an adviser who was a CFO through an exit. Whole Foods and others kept wanting his company on shelf, and they kept saying no. Turn them down and the offer gets better.

His bar is specific and I love it. Do not go into retail until at least half the room already knows your brand. Otherwise you are paying for awareness in the most expensive place there is, with bad sell-through waiting for you.

Albert told his own investors the same thing after the deal. He is not the guy to take Promix into retail right now.

The national Barry's partnership came the other way, through people he had known in New York for years. That is the whole mechanism.

The out of stock that cost him everything

Three pillars run the business. Make a good product. Make people aware of it. Get it to them.

Albert makes the product, Devin makes people aware, Albert and the COO get it delivered.

"I lost ungodly amounts of money because I went out of stock early on."

He is good at ops, and he was not doing real inventory forecasting or stocking agreements while running everything else. Every brand that has done well has an out of stock story.

What actually bothers him is the category it falls into.

"I don't get mad at things that happen. I get most mad if it's an unforced error."

Albert Matheny, Founder, Promix Nutrition

And on minimums: when you are small the MOQ is 20,000 units. "That can really F you up." Find someone who will make a few hundred first.

His bars are the proof. He turned a Rice Krispie treat over in the gym, thought about it, and built the puff bar. It sat there for three or four years. When the business finally grew, people started telling him they loved his new bars. "I've been dying on these bars for two years, no one knew about them."

13 people, and everybody knows whose job it is

Team of 13. He is deliberate about that.

Every single thing has a directly responsible individual, and he makes that excruciatingly clear in the room. Otherwise it does not get done.

Then he actually checks. "Okay, go ahead and pull it up. Did you do that?" Nice guy, extremely direct.

He is skeptical of big-name hires who have been comfortable for years, and openly allergic to people whose only skill is managing the people who do the work.

On the money side he quotes the rule straight: you should be default alive. If making payroll is a monthly question, the business is built wrong.

"We want to hold hands before we get married."

Devin, Promix co-founder, quoted by Albert

Contract first. Work together for a while. Maybe you do not even like each other.

And if your gut says "eh" on a candidate, that is a no. Hire when the answer is "this person is unreal."

What to take home

  • The meaty 40% is a positioning tool you can use tomorrow. The 1% already has a trainer and a coach. Build for the people trying to get healthier without rebuilding their whole life.
  • Write down the products you will refuse to make. Colostrum and gummies would both sell for Promix, and both are still a no. A filter that never rejects anything is not a filter.
  • Nate's one channel question is worth asking in your next meeting. Before you add a platform, prove you have exhausted the one already working.
  • Retail bar: wait until half the room knows your name. Otherwise you are funding awareness at the most expensive counter in the building.
  • Separate the unforced errors from the real ones. Out of stock is preventable, and on Amazon the algorithm makes you pay twice.
  • Hold hands before you get married. Contract with someone before you hire them, and never hire on "eh."
  • Ten years bootstrapped, 13 people, first order profitable, and he still formulates every product himself. That is the unsexy version, and it is the one that lasted. Growth Through Community.
